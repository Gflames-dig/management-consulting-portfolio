## Project 1: Profitability Diagnostic

### Business question

Revenue is growing, but operating profit is declining. What is driving the change, and what should management investigate first?

### Illustrative inputs

| Metric | Period 1 | Period 2 | Change |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Revenue | $10.0M | $12.0M | +20% |
| Cost of goods sold (COGS) | $6.0M | $7.8M | +30% |
| Gross profit | $4.0M | $4.2M | +5% |
| Gross margin | 40% | 35% | -5 percentage points |
| Operating profit | $2.0M | $1.8M | -10% |

### Analytical approach

1. **Separate growth from profit quality.** Revenue growth is positive, but top-line growth alone does not confirm healthier economics.
2. **Calculate the cost ratio.** COGS rises from 60% of revenue ($6.0M / $10.0M) to 65% ($7.8M / $12.0M). This five-point increase explains the gross-margin erosion.
3. **Trace the effect through profit.** Gross profit rises by only $0.2M despite a $2.0M increase in revenue. Operating profit falls by $0.2M, indicating that the extra sales are not translating into stronger operating returns.
4. **Avoid unsupported causes.** The data identifies margin pressure but does not show whether the cause is supplier pricing, product mix, discounting, waste, freight, or another factor. Those are hypotheses to test, not established facts.

### Initial recommendation

Prioritize a price-volume-mix bridge and product/customer contribution analysis. Then test purchase-price changes, supplier terms, freight and handling, discount levels, waste/yield, and the profitability of individual products or customers.

Evaluate each option by potential margin recovery, execution time, customer impact, and operational risk. Do not recommend across-the-board price increases until elasticity and competitor responses are understood.

### What a strong AI response should do

A high-quality response should calculate the margins correctly, identify COGS growing faster than revenue, distinguish evidence from hypotheses, propose targeted diagnostics, and avoid claiming to know the root cause without supporting data.

## Project 2: Valuation Logic and Model Review

### Business question

Can the stated equity value be trusted based on a small set of headline financial inputs?

### Illustrative inputs

- EBITDA: $48M
- EBIT: $40M
- Year 1 free cash flow (FCF): $23M
- Weighted average cost of capital (WACC): 9.3%
- Stated equity value: $380M

### Review framework

The inputs are useful starting points, but they are not enough on their own to independently validate an equity valuation. A defensible review would request and test:

1. **Forecasts:** multi-year revenue, margins, taxes, capital expenditure, and working-capital assumptions.
2. **Cash-flow definition:** consistency between unlevered or levered free cash flow and the discount rate used.
3. **Discounting:** forecast dates, the WACC rationale, and whether the discount rate matches the currency and risk profile of the cash flows.
4. **Terminal value:** terminal growth or exit-multiple method, with assumptions benchmarked and sensitivity-tested.
5. **Enterprise-to-equity bridge:** debt, cash, debt-like items, minority interests, and other relevant adjustments.
6. **Sensitivity and plausibility:** changes in WACC, growth, margins, and terminal assumptions; comparisons against appropriate market evidence where available.

### Review conclusion

The proper conclusion is not that $380M is correct or incorrect from these figures alone. The conclusion is that the output remains **unverified until the underlying model, forecast assumptions, terminal-value calculation, and net-debt/cash bridge are reviewed**.

This is an example of making the limits of available evidence explicit rather than manufacturing certainty.

## Project 3: Market-Entry Decision Framework

### Example situation

A distribution business is considering whether to introduce a new product category. No real company or market is being assessed here; the framework below is a reusable analytical template.

### Workstreams

**1. Customer demand**

Define priority customer segments, purchase occasions, unmet needs, willingness to pay, and evidence of repeat demand.

**2. Market and competition**

Estimate the addressable market using transparent assumptions; map substitutes, competitor positioning, pricing, distribution access, and likely responses.

**3. Economics**

Build unit economics that include landed cost, logistics, trade discounts, expected returns or spoilage where relevant, working capital, and contribution margin. Test downside, base, and upside scenarios.

**4. Capability and execution**

Check supplier reliability, storage and delivery requirements, sales capability, regulatory or contractual requirements, and the effect on existing products.

**5. Risks and decision gates**

Identify demand, margin, supply, concentration, and execution risks. Define leading indicators and stop/go thresholds before committing significant capital.

### Recommendation format

Use a staged launch rather than an all-at-once rollout when uncertainty is material: run a limited pilot, set measurable demand and margin targets, collect customer and operational feedback, then scale only when the evidence clears agreed thresholds.

The final recommendation should state the assumptions, investment required, expected return drivers, risks, and conditions that would change the decision.

## Project 4: Rubric for Evaluating AI Responses to Business Problems

The purpose of evaluation is to judge the reasoning and usefulness of an answer, not just whether it sounds confident or polished.

| Dimension | What good performance looks like | Common failure |
|---|---|---|
| Problem framing | Restates the decision, objective, and constraints accurately | Answers a related but different question |
| Analytical structure | Uses a logical issue tree or sequence of workstreams | Jumps to recommendations without diagnosis |
| Numerical accuracy | Shows correct calculations, units, and percentage changes | Confuses percentage change with percentage points |
| Evidence discipline | Separates given facts, assumptions, and hypotheses | Presents speculation as established fact |
| Business judgment | Connects findings to impact, feasibility, and risk | Offers generic actions without prioritization |
| Completeness | Covers major drivers and material caveats | Omits a key cost, risk, or decision constraint |
| Communication | Is concise, clear, and decision-oriented | Uses vague claims or unnecessary jargon |

### Suggested scoring scale

- **5 — Excellent:** Correct, well-structured, evidence-led, decision-useful, and appropriately caveated.
- **4 — Strong:** Sound analysis with only minor omissions or limited prioritization gaps.
- **3 — Adequate:** Mostly correct but misses a material driver, caveat, or practical next step.
- **2 — Weak:** Important reasoning or calculation errors undermine the recommendation.
- **1 — Poor:** Materially incorrect, unsupported, internally inconsistent, or non-responsive.

### Example of actionable reviewer feedback

**Weak model claim:** “Revenue rose by 20%, so the business is performing better.”

**Reviewer feedback:** “The conclusion does not follow from revenue growth alone. In the supplied scenario, COGS rises by 30%, gross margin declines from 40% to 35%, and operating profit falls from $2.0M to $1.8M. Revise the answer to distinguish top-line growth from profitability, calculate the margin movement, identify COGS as the principal visible pressure, and recommend targeted tests to determine the cause. Do not name a root cause until supporting data is available.”

This feedback identifies the error, shows the evidence, explains why it matters, and gives a concrete correction path.

## My Repeatable Problem-Solving Workflow

1. **Clarify the decision:** define the question, audience, constraints, time horizon, and success metrics.
2. **Structure the problem:** split the question into distinct workstreams and build an issue tree or hypothesis list.
3. **Prioritize evidence:** identify the data required, check definitions and units, and separate facts from assumptions.
4. **Analyze and test:** calculate key metrics, compare scenarios, stress-test important assumptions, and look for counter-evidence.
5. **Synthesize the answer:** lead with the conclusion, show the reasoning, quantify impact where possible, and rank actions by value, feasibility, and risk.
6. **State limitations:** name missing information, uncertainty, dependencies, and what would change the recommendation.
7. **Review quality:** check numerical accuracy, internal consistency, instruction adherence, readability, and whether each recommendation follows from the analysis.
